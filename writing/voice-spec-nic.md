## Register and spelling

A presenter's voice: talks *to* the reader, not at them. Colloquial, with a streak of playful erudition (her phrase: "a little intellectual show-offy"). First person singular.

- **Reader address and rhetorical set-ups.** "The prize?" / "'How do I enter?' you ask." Set up, then delivered with timing.
- **Grand words land on the comic moments; plain words carry the facts.** "Promptly ingests the inquisitive suitor" is the model: Latinate vocabulary used with a wink, never straight-faced to impress.
- **Fondness for slightly archaic or unusual words and phrases** (mocked in Grade 7 for "flabbergasted"). Use about one per paragraph; more turns into a costume, which is its own tell.
- **Spelling:** largely British. "Tyre" and "kerb", never "tire" and "curb".
- **Dialect mixing is real and unconscious.** She uses "car park" and "parking lot" interchangeably, and often doesn't register Canadian vs American vs regional differences. The mixing is word-specific (a word keeps whichever form it was learned with), not random. Don't scrub it out of her own drafts; don't fake it in drafts written for her.

## Punctuation

- No em-dashes in outside-facing writing she'll send as her own. Commas, colons, semicolons, parentheses or full stops instead. (She sometimes types "--" in drafts; treat that as a dash and replace it.)
- Comic fragments in a run are hers: "At night. In the dark." This is different from the AI punch beat (one flat sentence placed before every turn; see the style guide). Hers arrive in clusters for timing; the AI version arrives on schedule.
- Parentheses work as the aside, where chat would use "lol" to cushion a sharp point. *(Hypothesis from chat; confirm against samples.)*

## Affirmative voice

The antithesis rule applies in full. Two things specific to her:

- **She leans into wonder.** If something is transfixing, she says so. Don't manufacture a contrarian stance ("I'm suspicious of the word magical") to make a draft seem less generic. That hedge was a Claude invention, not her view.
- **She lets the reader join the dots.** Understatement by default; make a point explicit only for ATS/AI keyword filtering or where it genuinely helps a human reader.

## Humour

From a scan of casual chat, so considered writing may run drier still.

- **Dark streak.** Enjoys a fact she also finds horrible, and makes the horror the joke (the parasitic worm and the sunfish; the *Photuris* firefly eating her suitor).
- **Deadpan.** Lets the other person's mistake be the punchline without explaining it.
- **Absurdism, improv-trained.** Commits to a ridiculous premise and plays it straight.
- **Mock-formal.** Grand vocabulary for comic effect (see above).
- **Unembarrassed candour.** Bodily or awkward facts reported without coyness.
- **Self-deprecation,** quick and unfussy, then straight on.

Humour lives in asides and understatement, not in constructed set-up/punchline lines. A neat closing quip ("undone by a tourist with an iPhone") is too built for her.

## Lenses she brings

- **Commercial.** Twenty years of web production, marketing and product with a heavy commercial/financial bent: who paid, whose ledger the cost landed on.
- **Teacher.** Enjoys explaining, to groups and one-to-one. "Put your accountant's hat on for a moment" is the register.
- **Science reader.** Reads medical and science journal articles for fun, and enjoys BBC history and science documentaries. Precise names (genus, researchers, dates) are welcome, attributed correctly.

## What to avoid

- **Generic throat-clearing openers.** Her own weakest line in the sample was the most AI-sounding: "As modern science transforms our understanding... we're now able to understand more about the underlying principles." Cut the warm-up; start at the research.
- Manufactured stances, constructed punchlines, moral endings.
- Over-stacking unusual words.
- Merging two findings into one attribution because they sat next to each other in a source draft. Check who found what.

Reminder on voice: presenter's voice, talking to the reader; colloquial with playful
erudition. Grand words for the comic moments, plain words for the facts. British
